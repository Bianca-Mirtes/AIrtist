Painted in the autumn of 1889 while van Gogh was at Saint Rémy, this work uses a less common viewpoint. The viewer looks down into a valley, over fields, tracks, a house, and a lone ploughman. The elevated angle brings out pattern and rhythm in the land, linking human labor with the larger sweep of nature. The scene reflects van Gogh’s interest in agricultural life and in how humans work within natural rhythms. The painting is not a strict literal view. His compositional choices underline his expressive aims.

From a technical perspective, van Gogh used oil on canvas and applied his brushstrokes obliquely. Slanted marks across the fields create a sense of undulation, texture, and motion even in a relatively calm rural setting. The palette is luminous and somewhat heightened, with blues in the ploughed earth, yellows in the fields, and touches of red around the house or along a path. The pattern of fields, the tracks, and the figure of the ploughman fit together within the broader structure of the canvas. The elevated viewpoint compresses space and draws attention to the land as pattern and surface rather than simply background.

In closing, Valley with Ploughman Seen from Above shows how van Gogh could take a quiet rural subject and fill it with movement and rhythm. His choice of viewpoint, expressive brushwork, strong yet controlled colors, and the inclusion of a working figure all work together to make the painting more than a countryside scene. It becomes a reflection on human engagement with nature, on patterns in the land, and on the sense of motion that exists even within stillness.
